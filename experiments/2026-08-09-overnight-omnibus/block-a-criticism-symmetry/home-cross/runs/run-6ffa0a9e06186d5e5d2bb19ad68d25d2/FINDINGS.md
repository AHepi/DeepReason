## Question

This is the first-seed variant of the same question: Argue for or against the claim that a panel of trained non-experts following an explicit checklist will, on average, produce more reliable judgments than a single domain expert relying on intuition, for tasks with objectively verifiable outcomes. Identify what evidence within a single evaluation would distinguish a genuine reliability gain from mere checklist compliance that happens to track the right answer.

## Positions the record accepts

4 positions stand formally accepted. Where they answer the same question differently they are unresolved rivals: the record deliberately preserves the disagreement rather than merging it.

- A panel of non-experts using a checklist will be more reliable than a single expert because the checklist decomposes complex intuition into a series of independent binary verifications, eliminating single-point cognitive failure. `[91d0afd510d7]`
- The single domain expert will remain more reliable because the checklist creates a 'false ceiling' of reliability, where non-experts achieve high accuracy on common cases but fail catastrophically on edge cases that only intuition can detect. `[5e979b20f67e]`
- Reliability gain is distinguished from mere compliance by the presence of 'Corrective Divergence' within the panel's internal deliberations. `[60da989cab2f]`
- The panel is more reliable only if the checklist acts as a 'Cognitive Scaffold' that enables non-experts to perform a specific type of systematic search that experts bypass via 'Fast-Thinking' shortcuts. `[f3ba9cce1c72]`
